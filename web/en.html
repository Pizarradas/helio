A journey of 149.6 million kilometres

Heliofrom the core to the Earth

Every second, at the heart of the Sun, some 620 million tonnes of hydrogen turn into helium. About four million tonnes of that mass simply vanish: they become energy. This is the story of that energy: where it is born, how long it takes to get out and what reaches us.

01 Core · 0–0.25 R☉

The proton–proton chain

At 15.7 million kelvin and 150 g/cm³, two protons repel each other with a force their thermal agitation cannot overcome. They fuse anyway through quantum tunnelling: quantum mechanics lets them slip through the barrier. Even so, a proton waits some ten billion years on average to fuse. There are so many that the Sun shines all the same.

  1. p + p → 2H + e+ + νe

    The weak force turns a proton into a neutron: deuterium is born. Out come a positron and a neutrino, which will cross the Sun without touching a thing.

    +0.42 MeV

  2. 2H + p → 3He + γ

    Within seconds, the deuterium captures another proton. Helium-3 is born, along with a gamma photon that sets off on its own journey.

    +5.49 MeV

  3. 3He + 3He → 4He + 2p

    Two helium-3 nuclei collide and form helium-4. They hand two protons back to the plasma, ready to start again.

    +12.86 MeV

Net result: 4 p → 4He + 2e+ + 2νe + 26.73 MeV. 0.7% of the mass vanishes and becomes energy (E = mc²).

02 Radiative zone · 0.25–0.7 R☉

The drunkard’s walk

The gamma photon does not shoot straight out. The plasma is so dense that it absorbs it within a fraction of a millimetre and re-emits it in a random direction. Each time it leaves with a little less energy: gamma rays, X-rays, ultraviolet. It wanders like a lost walker, and its 300,000 km/s are of little use.

03 Tachocline and convective zone · 0.7–1 R☉

The Sun boils

At around 2 million kelvin, ions recapture electrons and the plasma turns opaque: radiation is no longer enough. Hot gas rises, cools at the surface and sinks again, like water in a pot. In a few weeks it carries the energy across the last 200,000 km.

Tachocline. The radiative interior spins like a solid body; the convective zone does not: the equator turns in about 25 days and the poles in about 35. In that shear layer, some 28,000 km thick, the magnetic field gets twisted: this is the solar dynamo.

04 Photosphere · 500 km thick

Light, at last, escapes

The photosphere is the layer where the gas turns transparent. It is covered in granules about 1,000 km across, each the top of a convection column that lives for 8 to 20 minutes. Sunspots are cooler patches, at about 3,800 K, where the magnetic field holds back convection.

05 Chromosphere and corona

Further out, hotter

You would expect the temperature to drop further out. It does not: above the photosphere it falls to about 4,100 K, climbs to 20,000 K in the chromosphere and, within a few hundred kilometres, leaps past a million in the corona. Why this happens is still one of the great open problems of solar physics.

06 Solar wind, flares and eruptions

The Sun escapes

The corona is so hot that gravity cannot hold it: it expands in every direction as the solar wind, at 300 to 800 km/s. Because the Sun rotates, the flow curves into a spiral (the Parker spiral). When the magnetic field reconnects, it releases a flare in an instant or hurls a coronal mass ejection: billions of tonnes of plasma.

07 The Earth · 1 AU

What reaches us

At 150 million kilometres, the magnetosphere deflects almost all of the wind. Some of it slips in over the poles, excites oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere and we see it as auroras. Times are worked out for the worst case: the Earth at aphelion and the slowest wind.
